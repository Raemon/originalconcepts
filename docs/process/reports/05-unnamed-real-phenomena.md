**Overview.** I couldn't find any pattern with zero precedent: every candidate has names inside individual fields. What I can defend as new is two things. First, a generalization across fields that, as far as my searches show, has no general name. Second, a game law that makes players reason in that pattern all the time. I rate my top 3 as roughly 60–70% built from known parts, and I list those parts for each.

## TOP 3

### 1. Stale Here (frozen indexicals / "frame fossils")
**Pattern.** A label coined relative to the speaker's here, now or facing ("new", "upper", "near", "left", "next", "modern") comes loose from that frame and survives as a fixed name. As the frame moves, the literal meaning drifts and often inverts. The surviving fossils together form a recoverable record of where and when the frame was.
**Instances.**
- Pont Neuf is the oldest standing bridge in Paris.
- New College, Oxford was founded in 1379.
- Near, Middle and Far East put London at the origin.
- Upper Egypt is the south, because the frame is the river's flow.
- Political left and right come from seating in the 1789 French Assembly.
- Human anatomy's "anterior/posterior" was inherited from four-legged animals.
- "Modern" architecture is now a closed period.
- A file called `report_new_FINAL2`.

**Predictions.** "New-" names sit disproportionately on old things. Inconsistent fossils triangulate the namer: "Middle East" plus "Far East" locate London. A dense crop of frozen comparatives means the frame moved a lot.
**Mental operation.** *Inverse deixis*: for each label, ask "from where and when was this true?", solve for the hidden origin, then translate into your own frame. Also *forward deixis*: coin names now that will still point correctly after your own frame moves.
**Closest named ideas.**
- Deixis, but deixis covers live words, not frozen ones.
- Retronyms, which run the other way.
- "Opaque" names in place-name studies.
- Lexical closures in programming, where a name captures its environment when it is defined. This is the most damning comparison.

About 60% known parts. The part that looks unnamed is the claim that fossils encode a frame's path, and that you can solve for it.
**Puzzles.**
- *"Meet at the New Gate."* There are four gates. Work out which was newest *to that signwriter*, using a "Second Bridge" sign that counts from a harbor that has since silted up.
- You leave yourself a label ("the near lever"). After a rotation or time-skip, the level reads it literally. Aha: name things by what won't change, or plan the inversion on purpose.
- Two "Upper Mill"s, where one meant upstream and the other uphill. The contradiction gives away the old riverbed.

**Visual.** Hand-lettered signs. Decoding a sign leaves a see-through ghost of its namer at their standing point, tinted by era, so the map slowly fills with past observers.
**Search.** I found no term that spans fields. "Fossilized deixis" and "frozen deixis" returned only literal fossils. A UN place-names manual notes names whose "signifiers and signifieds became detached." That is the local observation, without the idea of solving for the frame.

### 2. Margin Rot (repairs breed failures at their edges)
**Pattern.** Every repair creates a discontinuity, and its boundary becomes the likeliest place for the next failure. Repairing again there makes the repaired region grow outward. So the maintenance burden scales with total repair *perimeter*, not damaged area, and history piles up as rings of repairs.
**Instances.**
- Teeth: a filling fails at its margin, then come a bigger filling, a crown and a root canal.
- Roads: patches fail at their joints and turn into quilts of patches.
- Software: bug fixes spawn regressions right next to the fix.
- Welds: the heat-affected zone around a weld.
- Clothes: a patch tears at its edge.
- Plaster: a filled crack re-cracks beside the filler.
- Law: amended statutes get litigated at the seam between new and original text.

**Predictions.** Failure rate is proportional to seam length. Past some threshold, replacing a whole panel beats patching. Repairs whose edges line up with existing structural lines, or with each other, last longer.
**Mental operation.** Judge every intervention by its *edge*: where does my fix end, and is that a strong place for it to end?
**Closest named ideas.**
- Stress concentration, which is the physics of why it happens.
- Dentistry's "restorative cycle".
- Software's "regression cascade".
- Senge's "fixes that fail", which is broader and about side effects.

About 65% named, but only one field at a time.
**Puzzles.** A membrane takes periodic pressure waves. You place block-shaped patches, and their seams crack on the next wave.
- One big patch loses to two small ones whose edges follow the ribs.
- Overlapping a new patch onto an old seam *removes* that seam instead of adding one.
- Let a region tear on purpose, so its fracture becomes a clean line to align later patches to.

**Visual.** Material layers in distinct colors, seams glowing with strain, and cracks crawling along the seams on each wave. A finished level reads like tree rings.
**Search.** Dentistry has established terms: "restorative cycle", "restorative death spiral" and margin failure. Software uses "regression cascade" and "bad fix". Road-fault reports describe it ("the fault just develops next to the repair") but I found no term. I found no name that spans fields.

### 3. Counterweights (hidden mutual compensation)
**Pattern.** Two agents each correct for the other's correction, toward different targets. Their *settings* drift apart while the visible outcome stays flat. The gap is stored tension, released as overshoot when either one stops. Anyone who sees only one setting concludes that agent is absurd.
**Instances.**
- Heating on high with the window open.
- Padded estimates against budgets that always get cut.
- Grade inflation against employers who discount grades.
- Players faking fouls against referees who ignore contact.
- A clock set fast by someone who knows it's fast.
- Office climate zones heating and cooling at the same time.
- List prices inflated for people who haggle.

**Predictions.** The gap between settings grows over the pair's history. Removing one side causes an overshoot proportional to that gap.
**Mental operation.** Read every extreme as one end of a hidden spring: find its partner, then unwind the pair together or in a safe order.
**Closest named ideas.**
- Integral windup in control engineering, which is very close for machines.
- The Red Queen effect, which the user excluded.
- Arms races, except that there the outputs escalate, while here they stay flat.
- Offsetting behavior and the Peltzman effect, which are one-sided.

About 70% known. It is the weakest of the three on novelty but very playable.
**Puzzles.** A calm machine with every dial at an absurd extreme, where touching any one floods the room. You map the hidden pairings with small probes. Aha: a three-way compensation cycle that can only be released by adding a fourth counterweight for a while.
**Search.** I found no term. The nearest were "offsetting behavior" and "windup" in control engineering, when two loops fight over one actuator.

## The other 9
4. **Unaskability.**
   - Pattern: delaying a visible task creates a second task, explaining the delay, which grows until the original becomes impossible to do. Institutions invent amnesties to wipe it.
   - Instances: a colleague's name you never learned, email bankruptcy, library and tax amnesties, cover-ups.
   - About 55% known. Weak as a spatial puzzle.
5. **Find/make crossover.**
   - Pattern: when remaking is cheaper than finding, duplicates pile up. That raises search costs, which makes remaking even more rational.
   - Instances: phone chargers, duplicated utilities in codebases, re-derived lemmas, proliferating standards.
   - About 55% known ("not invented here", xkcd 927).
6. **Orphan drift.**
   - Pattern: things that only work in matched sets lose function faster than single units are lost, and the leftover orphans hide the remaining matches.
   - Instances: socks, Tupperware lids, earrings, the "marriage squeeze" in demography.
   - About 60% known.
7. **Index of mess.**
   - Pattern: a working mess records what was used when, and tidying deletes that record.
   - Instances: desk piles (Malone 1983), Noguchi filing, mise en place, irregular verbs surviving in frequent words.
   - About 70% known (move-to-front lists).
8. **Tense flip.**
   - Pattern: a live gauge becomes a record the moment it stops tracking.
   - Instances: stopped clocks at disasters, speedometer needle marks in crashes, max-min thermometers, Pompeii.
   - About 65% known.
9. **Float.**
   - Pattern: items in transit are counted by both parties or by neither.
   - Instances: check kiting, shipping risk-transfer rules (Incoterms), patients between wards.
   - About 70% known (banking).
10. **Peel vs. shear.**
    - Pattern: structures held by many bonds resist load applied all at once but fail to load applied one bond at a time.
    - Instances: Velcro, salami tactics, ripstop fabric, NATO's Article 5.
    - About 75% known (fracture mechanics).
11. **Last corner.**
    - Pattern: satisfying a loop of constraints one by one uses up the shared slack, so the last one starves.
    - Instances: the fitted sheet's fourth corner, a tire bead, star-pattern lug-nut tightening, seating plans.
    - About 70% known.
12. **Kill point.**
    - Pattern: a loop of compromises that doesn't close has to dump its error somewhere.
    - Instances: the "wolf" interval in tuning, the wallpaper kill point, the leap day, the date line, the jog in knitting.
    - About 85% known (holonomy, frustration). Rejected.

## Combination: Stale Here × Margin Rot gives **Datum Seams**
Both concepts are about two frames meeting.
- A frozen label is a *semantic* seam: coined in frame A, read in frame B.
- A repair margin is a *material* seam: built at time A, touching material from time B.

**World law.** Everything is built *and labeled* in its builder's current frame. **A seam is as weak as its two sides' disagreement about the frame**: where "up", "new", "zero" and "near" point. A mismatch in meaning is literally strain.

**New moves that neither thread has alone:**
- Reading misreadable signs as a stress map, and stress maps as history.
- Choosing where frame disagreements land, the way a decorator hides the wallpaper seam behind a door.
- **Re-datuming**: healing a seam by translating one side's frame instead of touching the material. The cheapest repair is a translation.

**Example.** Two crews patched a dam from opposite banks, each writing "UP" in their own frame. The crack runs exactly where "UP" flips. Patching it fails; relabeling one bank's reference point closes it.

**Real precedents** (a sign this isn't arbitrary):
- Laufenburg Rhine bridge, 2003. The German and Swiss halves used different sea-level references about 27 cm apart. The correction was applied with the wrong sign, and the halves met 54 cm out.
- Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999. One team's software reported pound-force seconds; the other expected newton-seconds.
- Ariane 5, 1996. An Ariane 4 velocity assumption, frozen into reused code, overflowed on the new rocket's flight.

Each is a frozen frame failing exactly at a seam. The nearest named idea is "interface error" or "impedance mismatch". Treating frame disagreement as a quantity you place, route and translate is, as far as I can tell, a new way of thinking.
